US refining in 2026 is a supply-shock story layered on top of a genuinely tight structural backdrop. The shock: Strait of Hormuz tension and strikes on Russian refining capacity have curtailed global product flows, pushing crack spreads to levels not seen in years and lifting every major refiner's earnings simultaneously. The structure underneath it: the US has not built a new refinery since the 1970s, two major plants (LyondellBasell Houston, Phillips 66 Carson/LA) shut in 2025 for a combined ~400,000 bpd of lost capacity, and demand has run ahead of capacity by roughly 400,000 bpd on average since 2023. Layered on top of both is a tightening Renewable Fuel Standard: the EPA's finalized 2026-2027 rule raised blending obligations sharply, pushing RIN prices toward 2021 highs and creating a real compliance cost for refiners without renewable-fuel capacity -- and a real profit opportunity for those with it (HF Sinclair, Valero's Diamond Green Diesel JV). The result is a sector where every major name is trading within single digits of its 52-week high after 50%+ quarterly returns -- genuine fundamental strength, but also a market that has already priced in a lot of the good news.

Porter's Five Forces

Supplier power (crude producers) — low-to-moderate: US refiners benefit from abundant domestic shale crude and advantaged feedstock access (Permian, Bakken), giving them a cost edge over import-dependent refiners elsewhere, though they remain price takers on global crude benchmarks.
MODERATE
Buyer power (wholesale/retail fuel buyers) — low-to-moderate: fragmented downstream buyers have limited leverage, and regional supply constraints (e.g. West Coast, which EIA flags as thin on inter-regional pipeline capacity) can give the local refiner real pricing power.
MODERATE
Threat of new entrants — very low: no new US refinery has been built since the 1970s given permitting, capital intensity, and environmental review; incumbents' capacity is effectively unreplicable at any relevant time horizon.
LOW
Threat of substitutes — low near-term, rising long-term: the US light-vehicle fleet remains overwhelmingly ICE today, but EV adoption and renewable fuels (which refiners are themselves capturing via RIN-driven renewable diesel/SAF segments) are a genuine multi-decade substitution risk.
MODERATE
Rivalry among incumbents — moderate: a consolidated set of majors (MPC, VLO, PSX) and near-majors (DINO, PBF, DK, CVI) control most Gulf Coast/Mid-Con capacity, and rivalry is currently eased by a tight, high-margin cycle where everyone is winning rather than fighting over share.
MODERATE

Net read: this is a classic high-barrier, consolidated-incumbent industry where the moat is regulatory/permitting scarcity rather than brand or technology -- which is precisely why the current margin windfall accrues almost entirely to existing capacity owners rather than inviting new competition.

Technology disruption

The main technology-driven shift in refining right now is not disruption of the core business but diversification into renewable fuels: renewable diesel and sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) production is forecast to grow production ~24% and biodiesel ~41% in 2026 versus 2025, and refiners with existing hydrotreating infrastructure (HF Sinclair, Valero via Diamond Green Diesel) can repurpose it for renewable feedstocks at a cost advantage over greenfield renewable-fuel plants. Longer-term, EV adoption and refinery digitization/automation for yield optimization are the two structural technology forces to watch, but neither is reshaping 2026 economics -- the cycle today is almost entirely a supply/demand and policy story, not a technology one.

Regulatory environment

Tailwinds: the finalized 2026-2027 RFS rule, while raising compliance obligations, is explicitly designed to grow domestic biofuel demand -- a direct profit driver for refiners with renewable-fuel segments, and the tight capacity backdrop gives incumbents effective pricing power without needing further regulatory help.
Risks: RIN prices near 2021 all-time highs mean refiners without renewable blending capacity face a real and rising compliance cost (AFPM estimates over $106B across the industry over two years), D4 RIN generation was only at 43% of the full-year obligation by midyear per S&P Global, and small refinery exemption (SRE) petition outcomes remain uncertain -- HF Sinclair is currently suing the EPA over delayed biofuel-blending decisions. None of this threatens the structural capacity-scarcity tailwind, but it is a real and current cost pressure that falls unevenly across the names in this report.

Emerging Opportunities

  • Underserved niche — renewable diesel/SAF as a bolt-on, not a bet-the-company pivot: HF Sinclair's renewables segment swinging from -$2M to +$123M adjusted EBITDA YoY shows refiners can capture RFS-driven upside with existing hydrotreating assets rather than needing greenfield investment.
  • Technology/capacity adoption gap — "capacity creep" over greenfield builds: with no new US refinery built since the 1970s, incremental de-bottlenecking and efficiency projects at existing Gulf Coast refineries are the only practical way to add US refining capacity, favoring incumbents with scale to fund such projects.
  • Regulatory tailwind for scale and renewables-capable players: the tightening RFS mandate and rising RIN prices favor refiners that already have renewable-fuel blending capacity (DINO, VLO) over smaller peers that must buy RINs on the open market to comply.
  • Consolidation targets: smaller independent refiners (PBF, DK, CVI) carry higher operating leverage to the cycle and thinner balance sheets, historically a precursor to being acquired by a major looking to add capacity cheaply (Marathon's own $35.6B Andeavor takeover is the sector's template) -- worth revisiting for a standalone small-cap target if the cycle cools and a specific deal rumor surfaces.
  • Regional supply-constraint niche — West Coast: EIA flags thin inter-regional pipeline capacity and 2025's Valero Benicia/Phillips 66 Carson closures as creating a structurally tighter West Coast product market for whichever refiner (PBF's Martinez among them) captures the displaced volume.

Key Risks Across the Theme

  • Crack-spread cyclicality and Strait of Hormuz de-escalation: the entire 2026 refiner rally rests substantially on a supply shock (Hormuz tension, Russian refinery strikes); a resolution, a demand-side recession shock, or returning OPEC+ supply would compress margins fast and hit every name in this report simultaneously.
  • RFS/RIN compliance cost burden: RIN prices near 2021 highs and a tightening 2026-2027 mandate create a real, rising cost for refiners without renewable-fuel capacity (AFPM estimates >$106B industry-wide over two years) -- a direct headwind for names like PBF/DK/CVI that lack a renewables segment.
  • Valuation already reflects the good news: every name covered here is trading within single digits of its 52-week high after a 50%+ quarterly rally -- the structural capacity-scarcity story is real, but most of the near-term re-rating has already happened.
  • Regional supply-chain fragility: EIA flags thin inter-regional pipeline capacity (especially West Coast) as a risk factor that can cause sharp regional price dislocations independent of the national crack-spread picture.
