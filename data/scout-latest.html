Market-size estimates for the neocloud category vary by 2x depending on scope (raw GPU-rental revenue vs. broader AI-infrastructure-as-a-service), but the demand drivers are consistent: hyperscalers are contracting directly with GPU-specialized cloud providers rather than building 100% in-house, and the constraint has visibly shifted this quarter from chip allocation to power and grid access. At the same time, this is now a capital-structure story as much as a demand story — every major neocloud (CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN) is funding multi-billion-dollar buildouts with debt secured against depreciating GPU collateral, and the sector's biggest open question is whether contracted revenue growth outpaces the depreciation/covenant clock.

Porter's Five Forces

Supplier power (Nvidia GPUs, and increasingly power/grid capacity) — high: Nvidia allocates scarce next-gen chips across hyperscalers and neoclouds alike, and as of this quarter, utilities and grid operators are becoming an equally binding supplier constraint as neoclouds race for gigawatt-scale power.
HIGH
Buyer power — high and rising: Microsoft, Meta, and OpenAI are simultaneously the neoclouds' largest customers and, in Microsoft's case, a direct in-house competitor (it declined to exercise a $12B CoreWeave expansion option in 2025, redirecting that capacity to OpenAI); large buyers can multi-source across CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN, and their own capex.
HIGH
Threat of new entrants — moderate-to-high: capital intensity is a real barrier, but capital is abundant (Nscale's 2026 S-1 filing, Crusoe's continued fundraising, IREN's crypto-to-cloud pivot) — the binding barrier is Nvidia allocation and power access, not access to capital itself.
MODERATE-HIGH
Threat of substitutes — moderate: hyperscaler in-house clouds (Azure, AWS, GCP) and custom silicon (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Microsoft Maia) reduce reliance on third-party Nvidia-GPU rental generally, and Meta's July 2026 admission of "excess compute capacity" shows a hyperscaler can turn from buyer to indirect competitor quickly.
MODERATE
Rivalry among incumbents — high: CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN, Lambda, Crusoe, and Nscale are all well-funded and racing for the same hyperscaler contracts, with the basis of competition itself shifting mid-cycle from GPU procurement to power/energy access.
HIGH

Net read: the highest-conviction way to play this theme without taking on any single neocloud's balance-sheet risk is the power/thermal-infrastructure layer (GEV, VRT) that every neocloud depends on regardless of who wins share; the neoclouds themselves (CRWV, NBIS, IREN) are demand-backed but genuinely leveraged bets on depreciation and covenant assumptions holding.

Technology disruption

Two shifts are compounding this quarter. First, the competitive basis is moving from GPU procurement to power procurement — CoreWeave (1.7 GW target), Nebius (4+ GW contracted), and others are now differentiating on gigawatt access and grid interconnection speed as much as on Nvidia allocation, which is why power-equipment suppliers like GE Vernova and Vertiv are directly leveraged to this theme without carrying neocloud-specific customer-concentration risk. Second, GPU depreciation economics are becoming a live financial-engineering question: rental/spot rates have fallen 50-70% from peak even as demand (measured in tokens processed) keeps climbing, meaning the useful-life and collateral-value assumptions embedded in billions of dollars of GPU-secured debt (most visibly CoreWeave's) are being tested in real time rather than remaining a theoretical risk.

Regulatory environment

Tailwinds: the January 2026 BIS rule shift moved H200/MI325X export-license review for China from presumption-of-denial to case-by-case (subject to a 25% tariff, a 50%-of-US-volume cap, and revenue-sharing with the US government), a modest loosening from 2025's outright restrictions that marginally eases global chip-supply tightness without removing the constraint.
Risks: that allocation regime remains politically contingent and reversible — a tightening, a Chinese retaliation, or a reversal of the case-by-case posture would cut the other way and re-tighten the chip-scarcity dynamic that currently underpins neocloud pricing power. Separately, no neocloud-specific regulatory regime (data-center siting, grid-interconnection permitting) yet rivals the SMR/nuclear sector's Part 53 reform in maturity, leaving power-access timelines largely a negotiation between neoclouds and individual utilities rather than a settled federal framework.

Emerging Opportunities

  • Underserved niche — power/thermal infrastructure, not GPU rental: every neocloud in this cycle's set (CRWV, NBIS, IREN) is now power-constrained rather than chip-constrained, which makes suppliers of gas turbines, grid equipment, and liquid cooling (GEV, VRT) a lower-variance way to participate in the same buildout.
  • Technology adoption gap — customer-concentration diversification: CoreWeave's Microsoft-revenue share has fallen from ~67% as OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, and Jane Street add capacity; Nebius's backlog is anchored by Meta and Microsoft directly; and IREN's pivot from crypto mining brings entirely new customers (NVIDIA itself, Microsoft, Perplexity) into the neocloud customer base — the market is still pricing these diversification profiles inconsistently relative to headline debt loads.
  • Regulatory tailwind, still politically contingent: the January 2026 China chip-export easing is a genuine, dated policy shift, but it is explicitly reversible and tied to an ongoing tariff/revenue-share negotiation rather than a settled framework — not yet fully priced into GPU-scarcity-dependent neocloud valuations.
  • Consolidation/capital-raising dynamics: Nscale's 2026 S-1 filing and IREN's rapid crypto-to-cloud pivot show new capital is still entering the category aggressively even as CoreWeave's debt/covenant risk draws scrutiny — a genuine test of whether the market rewards new, less-levered entrants over the incumbent leader.
  • Financing-structure divergence: CoreWeave's GPU-collateralized debt model contrasts with Nebius's more contract-backed balance sheet and GE Vernova/Vertiv's traditional industrial-equipment financing — a real, currently mispriced difference in risk profile across names that are otherwise lumped together as "AI infrastructure plays."

Key Risks Across the Theme

  • GPU depreciation & debt-collateral risk (sector-wide): GPU rental/spot rates have fallen 50-70% from peak, shrinking the collateral value backing billions in debt across CRWV, IREN, and (to a lesser extent) NBIS just as covenant tests and repayment schedules begin referencing that lower valuation.
  • AI compute oversupply / bubble debate: Omdia data supports a shortage narrative (token demand up from ~6M/min to ~15B/min in five months), but Goldman Sachs and a White House science adviser have both flagged a 2028-2029 compute-glut risk window, and Meta already admitted "excess compute capacity" in July 2026 — a direct threat to the capex-growth assumption behind every name in this set.
  • Nvidia chip export-control & tariff regime (China): the January 2026 BIS rule easing chip-export review for China is politically contingent and reversible; a reversal or Chinese retaliation would re-tighten global GPU scarcity, cutting both ways for pricing power and supply availability.
  • Customer concentration and buyer power: Microsoft, Meta, and OpenAI are simultaneously the neoclouds' largest customers and potential in-house competitors (Microsoft already redirected one CoreWeave expansion option to OpenAI in 2025) — a real risk to revenue durability across the group.
